Appeal Window Has Closed

This is not the end — the fight continues.

The deadline to appeal the move to ADAP was June 30, and the new rules block these appeals as of July 2. If you did not file one, please don't carry any guilt — there is another door, and it is still wide open.

That door is reassessment. If you cannot work because of a permanent disability, you can ask to be reviewed to return to AISH — and that path has no deadline. It does not close. More is coming soon on how to ask for a reassessment and put your strongest case forward.

⭐ Most popular — what people are using

Were you and your partner both paid at 88%? That rule is gone, and you are owed the difference

On 1 October 2026 the government made Order in Council 342/2026, filed the same day as Alberta Regulation 244/2026. It repeals section 8(4) of the AISH General Regulation — the rule that paid each partner 88% of the living allowance — and adds section 20(1.1), which says a client paid at the 88% amount is treated as underpaid for each of those months, and that the director must pay the difference.

The 88% amounts were $1,708 a month on AISH and $1,532 on ADAP. The full living allowance is $1,940 and $1,740. That is $232 a month on AISH and $208 on ADAP, for every month you were paid at the couples rate.

Request the money you were underpaid

The duty is on the government, not on you — the regulation does not say you have to apply. But it also does not say when, and there has been no public announcement. This letter puts your request in writing with a date, and asks for the payment, the calculation, and notice before anything is taken off it. Each partner sends their own letter, because the regulation speaks of each client separately. Keep every statement for the months you were paid at 88%.

Paid less than you were owed? Three forms, updated this week

If a payment came in short, the law requires the department to repay an underpayment in full, for the entire period. It does not matter whose mistake it was. Pick the one that fits what happened to you — they do different jobs, and you can send more than one.

The $200 CDB deduction Any underpayment — repayment request Formal notice — payment arrived short

Use the first if the flat $200 Canada Disability Benefit amount was deducted while you were not receiving the CDB, or were denied, or had more taken than you received. Use the second for any payment paid below what you were entitled to, whatever the cause. Use the third when a monthly payment arrived but the amount was wrong — a missing component, a deduction you do not recognise, or an amount that dropped with no notice. Ask for the calculation in writing, and keep the date you sent it.

Called in for an employment assessment? Read this first

AKG in the north and Serco in the south run employment services. They are not the people who decided you are disabled — a director did that, before you were ever sent to them. So taking part in the assessment and handing over your diagnosis are two different things, and you can do the first while declining the second. We read both instruments: no provision requires you to disclose your diagnosis to a contractor, and none ties a benefit consequence to declining. The consequences that are written attach to withholding information from the director, and to refusing employment supports — so take part, and keep those two lines apart when you speak.

Reassessment: the door that's still open

If you cannot work because of a permanent disability, you can apply to return to AISH. There is no deadline. As of July 2026 there's one combined application — the DS2444A, which you fill out, plus the DS2444B medical report your health professional completes.

Moved from AISH to ADAP? Application to go back to AISH New applicants — DS2444A Doctor's form — DS2444B Medical report guide Ask your specialist for a letter

New, October 6, 2026 — a shorter application if you were on AISH before. If you were moved from AISH to ADAP and you are applying to go back, you no longer fill in the whole form. On the page called Your Situation, near the front, tick Yes to “Are you a current ADAP client who transitioned from AISH to ADAP and want to apply for AISH?” Then complete three sections only: Information About You, Employment History, and Education/Training History. You can leave the spouse or partner, children, trustee, income and assets sections blank, because the program already has that information. You still need the DS2444B medical report. The form doesn’t say whether to sign the Declaration and Consents pages at the back; signing them does no harm. Make sure any copy you are handed says Rev. 2026-10 at the bottom — older copies don’t have the question. The government’s policy update is DIA-Program-2026-006.

New: your family doctor fills in the report, but a specialist often holds the findings that decide it. The specialist request asks for one of two things and says either will do — a short letter, or simply copies of the consult reports they already sent your family doctor, under the Health Information Act. The second needs nothing new written, which is why it gets said yes to. Send it to the clinic rather than the doctor; front desk staff handle record requests.

The medical report guide is the most-read document on this site — worth reading before your health professional fills anything in. Also: how to fill out DS2444A.

Asking for accommodation

If your disability makes part of the process hard or impossible — phone calls, travel, short notice, long forms, being asked things you cannot reliably answer on the spot — you can ask for it to be done differently, in writing. Asking is not refusing. A request for accommodation should never be recorded as a refusal to participate, and if they refuse or go silent, you hold written proof that the barrier was raised before anything was missed.

If your condition flares, read this

On ADAP, section 16(a) requires you to report when your employment is reduced or ends. Section 15(4)(a) makes "reduced or terminated" employment a ground to refuse, suspend, vary or discontinue your benefit. The regulation draws no distinction between someone who will not work and someone who cannot. There is no exemption for illness anywhere in it. This applies to ADAP only, not AISH.

Letter to your MLA

MS, lupus, ME/CFS, Crohn's, bipolar, long COVID, epilepsy, migraine, fibromyalgia, EDS — anything episodic. The power is discretionary ("may"), and we know of no case where it's been used. The point is that it exists and nothing is written against it.

On whether a section 15(4) cut can be appealed: this card previously said it could not. Our FAQ says it can. We have taken the claim down rather than leave two answers on one site, and we are re-checking it against the current regulation before we say either way. In the meantime, never accept a flat "you cannot appeal that" without asking, in writing, which exemption they are relying on.

Schooling & your child’s rights

What the school has to put in writing.

If your child has an Individualized Program Plan (IPP) at a public or separate school in Alberta, Grades 1 to 12, the plan has a list of things it has to contain. Those rules are not in the Education Act. They are in a provincial document called the Standards for Special Education, which Alberta Education still lists as current.

When a school’s answer has no date, no number and no name in it, you are allowed to ask for one. And if you get nowhere, there is an order to follow: the school’s own dispute process first, then an appeal to the school board, then a review by the Minister of Education.

The guides here walk you through it in plain language. They are not legal advice. Charter schools and kindergarten or preschool (Early Childhood Services) follow different rules.

Questions parents have asked

Real questions from parents, answered from Alberta's own rules. Information, not legal advice.

My child's IPP goals are vague, the same for three years, and have no baseline or assessment data. What do I do?

For example, a goal like “X will make connections between letters and sounds,” in its third year, with no way to tell when he’s achieved it. You’re right to push on it.

What the rules say

Alberta’s Standards for Special Education say every IPP must include:

  • assessment data
  • your child’s current level of performance
  • measurable goals and objectives
  • how progress will be evaluated
  • a formal review of progress at regular reporting periods

Alberta’s own IPP guide says “Without measurability, progress cannot be monitored,” and that each objective “should include a target date.” It also says goals, assessment data and current level of performance are things that usually change from one year to the next.

So a goal with no starting point, no target, no date, and the same wording three years running is worth questioning.

What a measurable version looks like

Instead of “X will make connections between letters and sounds,” something like:

“Right now, X can say the sound for 8 of 26 letters (tested [date]). By [date], X will say the sound for 20 of 26 letters when shown each letter. Checked every 6 weeks.”

That has a starting point, a target, a date, and a way to check.

What to ask, in writing

  1. What is his current level on this goal, and when and how was it measured?
  2. What exactly does “achieved” look like for this goal?
  3. What is the target date, and how often will progress be checked?
  4. What did the last two year-end summaries say about his progress on this goal?

If you don’t get answers by a date you set, take it to the principal in writing.

◆ Home Care & Self-Managed Care

Three ways to get home care. Most people are shown one.

Publicly funded home care in Alberta comes through three doors, and they are all part of the same system. Regular home care: the province arranges the workers. Client Directed Home Care Invoicing (CDHCI): you pick an agency from the Alberta Blue Cross list. Self-Managed Care (SMC): the funding comes to you, and you hire, pay and manage your own workers.

All three are now run by Assisted Living Alberta (ALA), the provincial agency for continuing care, which took over from Alberta Health Services. There is no age limit. Home care is not only for seniors.

To start: call the ALA Access Centre at 1-855-371-4122. Monday to Friday 8:00 am to 6:00 pm, weekends and holidays 8:00 am to 4:00 pm. Anyone can ask for an assessment, and you don’t need a doctor’s referral. After your assessment, ask about all three options by name.

Before you choose Self-Managed Care: you can’t pay family or friends with the funding, and signing the agreement makes you an employer, with payroll, CRA deductions and quarterly reports to ALA. No SMC rate is published anywhere, so keep your agreement and every letter.

Appeals

What Bill 12 actually changed.

Before December 9, 2025, an Albertan denied AISH could appeal to an independent body. Bill 12 removed that. Eligibility decisions for AISH and ADAP now sit with the Minister, with no independent reviewer between you and the program.

Read the next part carefully, because getting it wrong costs people their appeal. This does not mean there is no appeal. Eleven kinds of decision are shut out by the regulation; everything else can still be appealed, in writing, within 30 days. If you are told a decision cannot be appealed, ask in writing which instrument makes it exempt, and keep the answer.

My Individual Plan — Self-Advocacy Worksheet

When you move to ADAP, someone will call you about employment services and ask you to help build a plan. This worksheet means you don't walk in cold. Fill it in first, at home, on your own terms — as the expert on your own life. It has plain-language pages on what to expect and how to be heard, a breakdown of what your ADAP letter really means, the Alberta Supports numbers, and space to write your goals, what you can and can't do, the accommodations you need, the barriers in your way, and the letters that back you up — with an example for every section. Take your time; you can do one part a day, and ask someone you trust to help. It's yours to keep: bring the original to your meeting and let your worker take a copy. Three versions: fill on screen in colour, fill on screen printer-friendly, or print and write by hand.

MLA Letter — If Your Condition Flares (AR 96/2026 s.15(4))

You must report the very thing that can be used against you. Section 16(a) of the ADAP regulation requires you to notify a director when your employment ends or is reduced. Section 15(4)(a) makes "reduced or terminated" employment a ground to refuse, suspend, vary or discontinue your benefit. The regulation draws no distinction between a person who will not work and a person who cannot — there is no exemption for illness, incapacity, or a flare anywhere in it. "Reasonable employment" is never defined. And these decisions are exempt from appeal. This power applies to ADAP only, not to people who stayed on AISH.

If you live with MS, lupus, ME/CFS, Crohn's, bipolar disorder, long COVID, epilepsy, migraine, fibromyalgia, or EDS — anything episodic — this is aimed at your life. The letter quotes the regulation verbatim and leaves you space to say, in your own words, what a flare actually does to you. That part is the most powerful thing in it.

Request to Record a Medical Appointment

If you struggle to remember or process what's said at appointments, this asks your physician's consent to audio-record your own visit — so you have an accurate record. It follows the College of Physicians and Surgeons of Alberta's own guidance, which tells patients to explain their reason and ask consent first. Tick why you're asking (memory, cognition, anxiety, pain or medication, multiple providers) without naming a diagnosis. It also asks for fallbacks if recording is declined: a written summary before you leave, or a support person to take notes. Especially useful for a reassessment appointment, where getting the medical details right matters.

ADAP Employment Participation — Barriers Notice

A formal written notice to ADAP that your disability prevents you from taking part in employment programming, with a request for accommodation in writing. This is not refusing to participate — it's documenting that you're unable to. If ADAP grants accommodation, you get the exemption you need; if they refuse or don't respond, you hold written proof the program required participation and wouldn't address the barrier. Either way, the record is on your side. Fillable on any device, or print and complete by hand.

Exemption Category Correction Request

For anyone — or anyone you care for — in one of the four groups the government's own rules say automatically remain on AISH (a severe/profound developmental disability or PDD eligibility, a palliative or terminal condition, continuing care, or age 60+) who received an ADAP letter anyway. This is not an appeal — it's a request to correct an administrative error and to be told, in writing, who made the decision and why. Section 12.8 removed the appeal for the transition; it did not remove your place in these categories.

For when AISH or ADAP deducted the $200 Canada Disability Benefit amount but the deduction doesn't match the CDB status you reported — your application was denied and the $200 was taken anyway, it's still pending with no decision, or the status you reported wasn't recorded. Open letter

CDB Underpayment Repayment Request

For when AISH deducted the $200 CDB amount but you were not receiving the federal benefit — you were denied, your application is still pending, or more came off than you ever received. When AISH pays you less than you were owed, that is an underpayment, and the AISH General Regulation does not say "may" — it says the director must repay the outstanding amount for the entire period. This form states what was deducted, quotes the law that requires repayment, and asks them to put the calculation in writing and pay you back. It's the mirror of the repayment form: that one is for when you owe them — this one is for when they owe you.

The Legal Package

A complete, sourced package on the AISH-to-ADAP transition: the argument, the evidence, and the routes to act on it. It is built to be handed to a lawyer, a human-rights body, an international mechanism, a journalist, or an elected representative. The documents marked draft are working templates for completion by counsel; the dossier is the evidence they rest on. Everything traces to a primary source.

Content note. These documents discuss the health harms of work-conditionality policies, including suicide and the death of an Albertan. If this touches you personally, you are not alone — in Canada you can call or text 988 any time, or reach the Alberta Mental Health Help Line at 1-877-303-2642.
