## CHAPTER 1 — THE STERILIZATION TABLE (1928–1999)

### PRIMARY LEGAL SOURCES

**The Sexual Sterilization Act, SA 1928, c 37.**
Passed by the 6th Alberta Legislature, March 21, 1928. Royal assent March 21, 1928. Introduced by George Hoadley, Minister of Health.
Successor citations: RSA 1942 c194; RSA 1955 c311; RSA 1970 c341. Repealed 1972.
Original text accessible via Alberta Law Collection (Our Future Our Past project).

**The Sexual Sterilization Act Amendment Act, 1937.**
Removed the requirement of patient or guardian consent for persons classified as "mentally defective."

**The Sexual Sterilization Act Amendment Act, 1942.**
Second amendment. Expanded scope to additional diagnostic categories.

**The Sexual Sterilization Repeal Act, SA 1972.**
Repealed by the Lougheed government, 1972.

**Muir v. Alberta, 1996 CanLII 7287 (AB QB).**
Decided January 25, 1996. Madame Justice Joanne B. Veit, Alberta Court of Queen's Bench.
Plaintiff: Leilani Marietta Muir.
Damages: $250,000 general (pain and suffering) + $125,000 aggravated, plus prejudgment interest. Total $740,780 plus $230,000 legal costs.
Full judgment text accessible via CanLII: https://www.canlii.org/en/ab/abqb/doc/1996/1996canlii7287/1996canlii7287.html

**Bill 26: Institutional Confinement and Sexual Sterilization Compensation Act, 1998 (Alberta).**
Second Session, 24th Legislature. Introduced March 1998 by the Klein government. Withdrawn within 24 hours.
Bill text: section 3 ("Override of Charter") would have operated notwithstanding sections 2 and 7–15 of the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms and the Alberta Bill of Rights. Section 4 imposed a 180-day limitation period for further claims.
Bill text accessible via Alberta Legislative Assembly: https://docs.assembly.ab.ca/LADDAR_files/docs/bills/bill/legislature_24/session_2/19980127_bill-026.pdf

**United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (UNDRIP).**
- Adopted by the UN General Assembly September 13, 2007.
- Six articles referencing free, prior, and informed consent (FPIC): Articles 10 (relocation from lands), 11 (cultural and spiritual property), 19 (legislative and administrative measures), 28 (restitution for taken lands), 29(2) (storage of hazardous materials), 32(2) (project approvals on territory).
- Article 19 text: "States shall consult and cooperate in good faith with the Indigenous Peoples concerned through their own representative institutions in order to obtain their free, prior and informed consent before adopting and implementing legislative or administrative measures that may affect them."
- OHCHR overview: https://www.ohchr.org/en/indigenous-peoples/consultation-and-free-prior-and-informed-consent-fpic
- Canadian federal implementing legislation: *United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples Act*, SC 2021, c 14.

### THE STRUCTURAL CONTRADICTION (per *Information Asymmetry Plain Language Companion*, April 2026)

The Government of Alberta determined that 79,290 people have severe and permanent disabilities preventing them from earning a livelihood. The same Government of Alberta then designed a compliance system requiring those same 79,290 people to read forms written at Grade 12 to 16 reading level, complete multi-step paperwork chains across multiple agencies, find a family doctor in a province where 650,000 people have no family physician, pay physician fees out of pocket on a fixed income below the poverty line, file annual taxes with no refund and no taxable income, meet deadlines with no flexibility for medical flares or hospitalizations, and navigate phone trees, online portals, and appeal processes — all unaccommodated. The defining clinical features of the largest AISH populations (cognitive disorders, mental illness) include difficulty with sustained executive function, difficulty processing complex written communication, difficulty initiating and completing multi-step administrative tasks, and difficulty navigating institutional systems without external support. These are not aspersions; they are the diagnostic criteria. They are the basis on which the government approved each recipient.

### ALBERTA — Compassionate Intervention Act (Bill 53)

**Compassionate Intervention Act (Bill 53).** Legislative Assembly of Alberta, introduced April 15, 2025; received Royal Assent May 15, 2025. Provincial statute establishing the framework for involuntary treatment of persons with severe substance-use or addiction whose conduct is determined to be "likely to cause substantial harm to themselves or others within a reasonable time."

Key statutory provisions:
- Authority for adult family members, guardians, healthcare professionals, police, and peace officers to apply for an assessment order
- A three-person Compassionate Intervention Commission (lawyer, physician, member of the public, appointed by the provincial government) reviews applications and issues legally-binding treatment decisions
- 72-hour assessment-and-detoxification period; hearings before the full Commission required within the period
- Secure treatment orders up to 3 months in designated compassionate-intervention treatment facilities
- Community-based treatment orders up to 6 months
- Care-plan reviews every 3 weeks
- Replaces the 2006 Protection of Children Abusing Drugs Act (PChAD) for youth
- Expands beyond the existing Mental Health Act framework — operates against persons with severe substance-use or addiction even where the capacity test is met

**Infrastructure investment.** Budget 2025 allocated $180 million over 3 years; Budget 2026 increased the allocation to $291 million over 3 years (2026-2029) for the construction of two 150-bed compassionate-intervention centres in Edmonton and Calgary. Construction beginning 2026; centres expected to open by 2029. Approximately 100 secure beds being established in existing facilities for the implementation phase. **Recovery Alberta** operates as the provincial health agency administering the framework.

**Mental Health Act, RSA 2000, c M-13.** The existing provincial involuntary-treatment framework, operating against persons with mental disorders meeting the impaired-capacity test. The CIA expands beyond the MHA framework.

## CHAPTER 7 — CONSENT WAS NEVER THE POINT

### THE FOUR-MOMENT ARC

This chapter draws on the full source apparatus of all six prior chapters. It does not introduce new primary sources beyond a small number that bridge the historical arc. The principal additional source category is the **1937 amendment to the Sexual Sterilization Act**, which removed the consent requirement for persons classified as "mental defectives." That provision is the textual evidence that even within the original framework, consent was substitutable from the start.

### SOURCES NEW TO CHAPTER 7

**Sexual Sterilization Act, SA 1928, c 37.** As originally enacted (carried forward from Chapter 1).

**Sexual Sterilization Act Amendment Act, SA 1937, c 47.** Statute of Alberta, 1937. Amended the original 1928 framework to remove the patient-consent requirement for persons classified by the Eugenics Board as "mental defective" — that is, persons whom the Board's own framework determined could not validly consent. The 1937 amendment is the textual instrument by which the consent requirement was removed from the framework, eight years after the original Act, and is the legislative moment at which the framework's own consent provision was abandoned.

Sources cited in: Wahlsten, D., "Leilani Muir versus the Philosopher King: Eugenics on Trial in Alberta," *Genetica* 99 (1997): 185-198; Park, D., and Radford, J.P., "From the Case Files: Reconstructing a History of Involuntary Sterilization," *Disability & Society* 13(3) (1998): 317-342; *Muir v. Alberta*, [1996] AJ No 37 (QB), Veit J., on the operation of the consent provisions and their abandonment.

**Sexual Sterilization Act Amendment Act, SA 1942, c 48.** Statute of Alberta, 1942. Further amended the framework to expand the categories of persons subject to its operation, including persons with neurosyphilis, epilepsy, and Huntington's chorea, among others. The 1942 amendment is the textual instrument by which the framework's reach was extended beyond the original 1928 categories.

**Sexual Sterilization Act Repeal Act, SA 1972, c 87.** Statute of Alberta, 1972. The Lougheed government's repeal of the Sexual Sterilization Act. The Act was repealed without formal acknowledgment of the harm produced by the framework's 44-year operation, without compensation to those sterilized under it, and without consultation with survivors about institutional and policy successors. The Eugenics Board was disbanded; the institutional framework within which it operated continued in modified form into the period documented in Chapter I.

### THE THREE MODALITIES OF CONSENT SUBSTITUTION (developed in this chapter from the prior record)

The documented record across the four moments demonstrates three distinct rhetorical-and-legal mechanisms by which "consent" has functioned as a rhetorical structure rather than as a substantive standard:

1. **Substitution within the framework.** The 1937 amendment substituting Board authorization for individual consent when the Board itself classified the individual as incapable. This is consent-substitution by statutory design — the framework providing within its own terms for the consent requirement to be operationally bypassed.

2. **Override by external instrument.** The 1998 Bill 26 attempt to invoke section 33 of the Charter to override Charter-protected rights of sterilization survivors. This is consent-substitution by constitutional override — the use of an external legal mechanism to bypass the rights the consent standard would protect.

3. **Substitution through procedural framing.** The 2025 Bill 12 framework, in which the consultation standard is claimed to have been met through a process that did not meet the substantive elements of the standard (representative organizations, accessible information, ongoing engagement, design-phase input). This is consent-substitution by procedural framing — the operational form of consultation deployed in the absence of its substantive content.
